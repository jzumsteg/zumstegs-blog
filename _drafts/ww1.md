I call World War I the "forgotten war" in the United States, because the U.S. was in it for only about 18 months. Our presence was decisive, not because our soldiers were so much better or braver, but because the two sides - Germany versus France and Britain - had spent four years beating each other to a pulp to the point where they simply could not wage war any more. The United States entered the war in April, 1917, and by Spring 1918 were sending 10,000 troops <I>per day</i> to France. In the final offensive of the war, from April to November of 1918, the fresh American soldiers overpowered the decimated and worn out German forces. It took only six months.

Here's the reason American troops were so important: in the previous four years, France lost 1,357,800 men, Britain 908,000 and Germany 1,774,000. The western front combatant nations simply had no troops left to fight with. 

The Battle of the Somme launched on July 1, 1916. British and French forces attacked the German lines. Generals believed they would accomplish a breakthrough the first day - the second at the latest; they believed heavy artillery shelling would have destroyed the German defenses. Hoo, boy, were they wrong. On the first day the British forces lost 19,640 men - the largest death toll in the history of the British army. They accomplished essentially nothing. For months later winter stopped the offensive; the lines of the front had moved just a few miles ikn one area, and not all all in others.

I thought we would be able to see the battle sites of the Somme, but I was wrong. I learned that there is nothing to see: the sites are now farms. The villages - totally destroyed during the war, have been rebuilt. Where the battle completely destroyed forests, the 110 years since the battle have allowed the forests to return. There really are no reminders of the battles that raged here.

What's still visible? Monuments to the soldiers and graveyards. Britain chose to bury its soldiers near where they fell. As we traveled through the area, we came across many small cemeteries -  a couple dozen to a couple hundred graves. I read that there are over a twenty-nine hundred British cemeteries in France holding British soldiers who were killed in World War I. 

What also is present: memorials to the soldiers. The awful thing is that all of them contain many, many graves marked "A soldier known but to God." Over 50% of the soldiers killed on both sides were never identified; most were not ever found.

If you're at all interested in seeing war sites, my advice is: go to pay tribute to the bravery and courage and patriotism of the soldiers, and to curse the idiocy of their leaders, and to think about the waste of war.

I wish I could find some profound words to sum up the experience of seeing these memorials and cemeteries. I cannot, really. Maybe the best is an overriding emotion: "What a waste."
